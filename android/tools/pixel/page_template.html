Debubble Room

Your room mirrors your real body, room and heart, and today's one mission outside is the letter in your hand. Tap yourself, your heart, the mess or the letter to start that task. Doing the real thing cleans the room and earns something for it. You start with nothing. Not now? The bell beside "I did it" reminds you at a time you pick. After you move in, a short tour shows you around; "Show me around" replays it. This is the app's engine running in the browser.

Sealed room

Drag anything, including yourself; your heart and the letter come with you. Floor things stay on the floor, wall things on the wall. Tap to select.

Day 1

"Do a month" plays thirty days of the three care tasks at tiny or standard size and today's one mission outside, so you can see the room fill up and the walls open.

On your home screen

the widget, from the same state

The widget is the room and today's four. Each row leads with its moment, because a plan works when it is in front of you at the moment it names. Tapping a task opens it in the app; there is no tick-box that skips the app. Shorter, it becomes a strip of icons under the room.

One day, one list

Body, room, heart, and one mission outside: the same four every day. A short fixed list removes the "what should I do?" that stops people on a low day, and four boxes filling in pull you toward the end of the set (the goal-gradient effect; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006).

The room is a mirror

Seeing your own progress is one of the best-supported ways to change behaviour, especially when the record is physical and visible (Harkin et al., 2016, 138 studies). The mess is capped, cute, and never takes away anything you earned.

Habits need sameness

Automaticity comes from repeating the same act at the same moment (Lally et al., 2010; Wood & Rünger, 2016). So each care area has one routine you keep, at one cue. What changes day to day is its size, never the act. A tiny version counts in full.

Exposure needs one thing, done properly

One mission a day, from ladders that climb slowly, with the moment, the first two minutes and the safety behaviour to drop. The other two ladders are there as extras. Missions open the walls and bring things from the world.

Make it yours

You pick how you look when you move in. People keep going longer with a character they made to look like them (Birk et al., 2016), and value what they built over what they were handed (Norton et al., 2012).

Not now? Then when.

A plan only works if it is remembered at the moment it names, and remembering to do something later is what fails most. So the moment you decide "not now" is the moment the app asks "then when?" — a bell beside "I did it". Reminders help people repeat things (Fenerty et al., 2012); the cue is what makes them stick (Stawarz, Cox & Blandford, 2015).

Trying counts

A no, a silence, turning back: each is a spark in your courage jar, because approaching the thing is what exposure rewards, whatever comes back. And the guess you write before going is checked after, because a fear that goes unchecked is never disproved (Craske et al., 2014).

Missing a day is not a reset

In Lally's study one missed day did not set habits back, so there is no streak to break. After a gap the app says welcome back and suggests the smallest version (Breines & Chen, 2012).

The routines

Every routine has three sizes of the same act, the mechanism in plain words, where the evidence comes from, one change to your surroundings that makes it easier, and moments it can hang on.
